Two conclusions follow, and neither is flattering. The decision layer is leaving real

large one. Both statements are measured here, not asserted.

### What this does and does not measure

**It is a lower bound.** Ten violation codes are implemented and tested; only the
time-derived ones can bind here. TRAI quiet hours are adjudicated from the episode's own
`occurredAtMs`, which the world has always planted. DLT registration, WhatsApp opt-in and
service window, and the RBI e-mandate pre-debit/AFA facts are **granted** to the arm by the
harness, because the simulator plants no such facts and a fail-closed gate on absent
metadata would refuse every contact — measuring the simulator's silence, not the
regulation. Planting those facts is a world change and is deliberately not folded in here.

So the true cost of compliance is **at least** the figure above, and the gap is one
this report cannot currently size.

## Hypothesis: was the shortfall an action-space problem?

The prior diagnosis: `calculateEir` scored only the single action a deterministic
proposer had already picked, making EIR a **veto rather than an argmax** — so when
contact was too risky the fallback became *nothing* rather than the cheap silent
action still worth taking. The falsification condition was stated in advance:
*argmax must beat Baseline with a paired 95% CI excluding zero on ≥18/20 seeds.*

Argmax over the feasible set, with real per-customer caps, is wired. Result: **₹2,95,124 vs Baseline, 20/20 seeds.**

**The hypothesis is still falsified — read the attribution carefully.** Argmax alone
did not close the Baseline gap; it was measured at 0/20 when it shipped alone. The
sign flipped only once a **contact-fatigue term** entered the churn calculation, which
is a feature-coverage fix, not an action-space one. Crediting the argmax for this win
would be exactly the post-hoc attribution the pre-registration existed to prevent.

no yardstick, because everyone trusts it.

**So there is real money here and we are not capturing it.** That is a harder finding
than the one this report used to carry, and it makes deliberate exploration on our own
suppression boundary urgent rather than academic: the variable we call unobservable is
one we have never tried to observe.

### Headline: incremental recovery per seed

Method, exactly: across-seed 95% Student-t interval over 20 per-seed point estimates (df=19, t=2.093). Each per-seed point estimate is
(rate_treatment − rate_holdout) × mean treated amount × n_treated, computed on one
independent world. The 20 points are the replicates; sd = ₹6,84,191, se = ₹1,52,990.

This interval replaces an earlier one that averaged the *endpoints* of 20 separate
per-seed bootstrap intervals. An average of endpoints is not an interval and has no
coverage property; it was also labelled with a method that had not been applied to it.

### Per-seed interval (within one world)

Each seed also carries its own interval. Method, exactly: nonparametric cluster bootstrap, 10,000 resamples of customers (not episodes) with replacement, carrying each episode's actual amount; percentile interval.
Median per-seed 95% CI width across 20 seeds: **₹20,80,195**.
Seed 1 for illustration: ₹28,33,954 [₹17,54,835, ₹38,92,755], 
7,542 customer clusters, 26,895 treated / 1,366 held out.

The bootstrap resamples **customers, not episodes**, and carries each episode's actual
amount. Episode amount is a per-customer constant drawn from a heavy tail and there are
~6 episodes per customer, so episodes are neither independent nor exchangeable. The
previous version resampled Binomial(n, p̂) *counts* and multiplied by a constant mean
amount, which assumes away both facts.

Honest accounting of what that change bought: for the **rate** estimator, almost nothing
— the clustered interval is ~1.02× the width of the old parametric one (median width ₹20,80,195).
The rate form multiplies by a constant mean amount by construction, so it never exposed
the amount variance in the first place, and within-customer correlation in *recovery* is
weak. The variance the old bootstrap was hiding shows up in the **revenue** estimator,
whose intervals are 1.92× as wide (median ₹39,98,266). The old method was wrong in principle
and, on the specific quantity it was applied to, close to the right width by accident.

This is the only evidence that any of the numbers above mean anything. The world plants
the true post-action and native recovery probabilities for every episode, so the true
incremental revenue on the treated episodes is known exactly:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;`truth = Σ over treated episodes of (p_realized − p_native) × amount`

Mean planted truth: **₹25,45,730 per seed** 
(vs. estimated ₹24,60,033).

| Check | Covered | Coverage | Mean relative bias of the point estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate estimator's CI contains the **planted truth** | 17/20 | **85.0%** | -3.4% |
| Rate estimator's CI contains its **own estimand** | 17/20 | 85.0% | -1.6% |
| Revenue estimator's CI contains the **planted truth** | 19/20 | **95.0%** | -3.3% |

Row 2 isolates the bootstrap: the estimand is E[Θ̂ | assignment], computed from the
planted probabilities. If row 2 covers and row 1 does not, the interval is fine and the
*estimator's target* is not the money. If neither covers, the interval is too narrow.

Rows 1 and 3 are two different estimators of the same planted truth:

- **Rate** (the headline, and what the product reports): (rate_T − rate_H) × Ā_T × n_T.
  Multiplying a rate difference by the *mean* amount equals the incremental rupees only
  if lift and amount are uncorrelated. Measured mean bias against planted truth:
  -3.4%, 95% CI [-16.0%, +9.2%] — not distinguishable from zero at this sample size.
- **Revenue**: (mean_T[recovered × amount] − mean_H[recovered × amount]) × n_T. A plain
  difference-in-means on rupees per episode, with no independence assumption between
  lift and amount, and the estimator that actually targets the amount-weighted truth.
  Measured mean bias: -3.3%, 95% CI [-20.7%, +14.2%]. Its per-seed intervals
  are 1.92× as wide, because carrying real amounts through the resample is where the
  heavy tail shows up.

Both bias intervals contain zero, so neither estimator shows a detectable bias over
these seeds — which is a statement about power as much as about the estimators. The rate form is kept as
the headline because it is what the product computes; the revenue form is reported
beside it because it is the one whose estimand is unambiguously "rupees recovered".

Revenue estimator, across seeds: **₹24,66,202 per seed, 95% CI [₹20,14,857, ₹29,17,547]** (across-seed 95% Student-t interval over 20 per-seed point estimates (df=19, t=2.093)).

Nominal coverage is 95%. With 20 replicates, two binomial standard errors put the
"not a failure" floor at 85.3% (18/20). Row by row:

- planted truth (rate): 17/20 — **BELOW the floor**
- own estimand (rate): 17/20 — **BELOW the floor**
- planted truth (revenue): 19/20 — **clears the floor**

The estimand row sitting on or under the floor is the honest signal to read: the
per-seed cluster-bootstrap percentile intervals are, if anything, marginally too
narrow — not too wide. The misses are driven by a small number of seeds in which a
few very large-amount customers dominate the treated total; the percentile bootstrap
handles that tail imperfectly. Nothing has been tuned to move this number.

### Randomization unit (SUTVA)

Contact fatigue, message caps and voice caps are all **per customer**. If the holdout is
randomized per *episode*, two episodes of one customer can land in opposite arms: the
randomization unit is then not the interference unit and the holdout arm is not a clean
counterfactual. These numbers are produced under **customer-level** randomization; the
episode-level row measures what the other choice would have cost.

Both units were run on the identical worlds, so the difference in bias is **paired**:

| Randomization unit | Mean bias vs planted truth | 95% CI | Mean \|bias\| | Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| episode (sensitivity probe) | -11.6% | [-20.5%, -2.7%] | 19.6% | -39.6% … +26.4% |
| **customer** (used above) | -3.4% | [-16.0%, +9.2%] | 19.1% | -66.0% … +42.5% |

**Paired difference (episode − customer): -8.2%, 95% CI [-23.5%, +7.1%].**

**That interval contains zero: over these seeds the choice of randomization unit does not move the estimate by a detectable amount.** That is a measurement, not an endorsement of episode-level assignment.

There is a specific, checkable reason the effect on *this* estimand is muted. In
`lib/simulator.ts` contact fatigue enters only (a) the lift of an *executed contact
action* and (b) the churn hazard. It does **not** enter `nativeProbability`. A held-out
episode resolves at its native probability, which nothing done to that customer's other
episodes can change — so the recovery-rate estimator has a weak interference channel to
be biased through. The interference that does exist runs through churn and through
cap-driven eligibility, and this estimator measures neither. An outcome that *is*
fatigue-mediated (churn, LTV) would not be protected by that accident.

Per-seed bias is dominated by estimator noise under both units: sd 26.9% (customer) and
19.1% (episode), around means of -3.4% and -11.6%.

Coverage of the planted truth: **17/20** customer-randomized vs **20/20** episode-randomized.

Assignment integrity check on this run: **0** customers had eligible episodes in
both arms, across all seeds. Under customer-level randomization that number must be 0.
It is recomputed on every run (`customersSplitAcrossArms` in the JSON) and is an
assertion about the code that produced these numbers, not an estimate.

The episode-level row is a **retrospective sensitivity probe**: the harness re-draws the
arm at the episode level on exactly the episodes the policy admitted to randomization,
honouring the same ₹50,000 value cap. It is not what the shipped code does.

## Policy calibration status

The world contains **repeat customers** (~1 per 6 episodes, several failures each over a
billing timeline) with contact fatigue: each prior contact from the same arm decays the
next intervention's lift and raises churn hazard. Contact is therefore genuinely scarce.

- The churn term in EIR is computed from dormancy alone and is blind to contact history.
  `previousInterventionCount` exists on the customer profile, is hardcoded to 0, and is
  read by nothing. A global `churnAversion` multiplier cannot stand in for a per-customer
  signal; the fix is a fatigue term in EIR, not a bigger multiplier.
- Read the Oracle row above before reading anything else into the arm comparison. The gap
  between RecoverOS and Oracle is how much of the loss is the decision layer's fault; the
  gap between Oracle and Baseline is how much value intervention can create in this world
  at all.

**`dormancyChurnScale` crosses zero at approximately 1.74×** — between
`1.5` (+₹12,585) and `1.75` (−₹538).

**Read the direction carefully, because it is the opposite of the intuition.**
RecoverOS loses as churn gets *more* expensive, not less. Baseline is silent retry:
it contacts nobody, so it pays no churn at any scale and is structurally immune to
this axis. RecoverOS contacts, so every rupee the world adds to the churn hazard is
charged to us and not to our comparator.

So the operating window is bounded on **both** sides, and neither bound is comfortable:

- Below it, churn is cheap enough that indiscriminate contact pays and the naive Rules
  arm — which we beat by a wide margin here — is the right product instead of this one.
- Above it, contact is so expensive that the correct policy is to contact *nobody*, and
  RecoverOS is not restrained enough to find that. Baseline is, by accident.

The headline result therefore rests on the world's churn hazard being within roughly a
factor of two of what `SIMULATOR.md` assumes. That assumption is the load-bearing one
in this repository, it is ours, and this is the sweep that says how much weight it carries.

### Known limitations of this run

- Outcomes come from a synthetic world with planted ground truth (`lib/simulator.ts`,
  assumptions in `SIMULATOR.md`). The generative model is a different functional shape
  from the scorer, but it is still our own world. Coverage against planted truth says the
  estimator is sound *given this world*; it says nothing about whether the world is right.
- The Oracle knows each episode's true post-action probability and its churn draw in
  advance. It is an upper bound no achievable policy reaches, not a target.
- The holdout estimand is incremental revenue on TREATED episodes only. It is not a
  program-wide effect: episodes the policy declines to touch never enter randomization.
- `ESCALATE` is modelled as a human review that runs, costs ₹110 and earns the planted
  human-agent lift, outside the automated contact budget and carrying no contact-fatigue
  churn. Previously it was billed on episodes where nothing executed while still
  collecting the lift for free; that is fixed, and this is the choice that replaced it.
